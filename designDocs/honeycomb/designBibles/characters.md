Character Bible: Designing new characters, items, and unlocks.

Reading the Mechanical Bible is mandatory for understanding this material.

# Unlock Categories & Benefit Profiles

Unlock mechanisms are defined by their scope, visibility, and persistence. Each category serves a distinct functional role in how it introduces power to the player.

### Abilities
Definition: Special limited-use abilities intrinsic to each character, split into two parts:
Ability 1: Low cost, limited use, refreshes each combat. Can serve as an enabler to the character's primary mechanic to reduce the risk of "dud" hands.
Ability 2: High cost, highly restrticted, requires a specific playstyle or mechanic to enable. Functionally serve as alternatives to rare cards to reduce the impact of poor luck during a run.
Allowance: Totally unlimited. Characters carry their abilities with them in all circumstances except when another unlock disables them for some other benefit or replaces them to serve some other purpose. 
Benefit Level: Low. Micro-consistency and baseline tuning. Nodes offer incremental stat increases, small friction dampeners, and agency tools (like card rerolls or innate tag assignments) that smooth out mid-run variance. Any large or exciting benefits should come with equal trade-offs, like blocking off alternate routes.

### Character Progression Tree Nodes
Definition: Individualized, branching node grids unique to each character, leveled up over time using persistent meta-currency.
Allowance: Mostly unlimited. Players will be expected to build the progression trees along the course of the game, with only some individual limits on what nodes are exclusive with each other. Thus each node must be considered in tandem with the rest, and all nodes should be designed with the mindset of "Is it a problem design-wise if this is enabled forever?".
Benefit Level: Low. Micro-consistency and baseline tuning. Nodes offer incremental stat increases, small friction dampeners, and agency tools (like card rerolls or innate tag assignments) that smooth out mid-run variance. Any large or exciting benefits should come with equal trade-offs, like blocking off alternate routes.

### Common Relics & Heirlooms (Equippable Starters)
Definition: Persistent passive items added to the player's available loadout pool before a run begins. Relics are usable by any character; Heirlooms are strictly bound to a specific character. Common relics & heirlooms are unlocked on obtainment and equippable from the start, whereas rare relics & heirlooms vanish when the run ends.
Allowance: Mostly exclusive. Players can equip additional relics in a smaller party, but are mostly limited to a singular choice from the pool. Thus each relic can have a sizeable impact on gameplay.
Benefit Level: Medium. Run-wide baseline rules and safety nets. These provide predictable, steady passive utility—such as early-combat stabilization, economy nudges, or draft-filtering mechanics—that lower run volatility. Things the player will be glad to have, but are mostly interchangeable.

### Outfits
Definition: Visually distinct cosmetics unlocked through milestones that alter a character's starting card configuration or intrinsic tactical posture, switching the character between sister mechanics.
Allowance: Totally limited. A single costume per character, often with drawbacks and benefits which completely change the character's gameplay.
Benefit Level: High. Ranging from primary shift in starting deck identity to goals of the team's final strategy. Outfits adjust the character's baseline playstyle, starter hooks, or replace abilities, allowing a single character to feel fundamentally different at the start of Act 1 without forcing them into a pre-determined Burst or Grind build.

# The Meta-Progression Design Space

This list represents the complete catalog of mechanical levers available for meta-progression, independent of which specific unlock mechanism delivers them. Essential to note, this is specifically the design space for starting tools, which are NEVER meant to decide on and build the player's final strategy for them. Thus they primarily serve to make the early game more managable, leading to more time and resources to prepare for late game.

### 0. Ability Allowance & Central Identity Establishment
Basic Ability: Unlocks the character's first ability, giving them a consistent tool for enabling their mechanic or adding potential fuel for a combo.
Removing Training Wheels: Replaces the character's first ability or grants it an additional highly situational effect, giving players the ability to gamble away their consistent low-quality enabler tool for something specific they think the build needs.
Core Identity: Unlocks or replaces the character's second ability and overarching unique mechanic, giving them access to a pseudo-rare card effect with high reliability.
Pathway to the Ultimate: Unlocks the mid-run conditions for enhancing the second ability further, providing a dedicated character-specific build path where the second ability becomes their primary build-around.
Tag Changes: Alters or edits the characters tags, mostly for flavor, it's super cool that Nettle's tag can change even if it does nothing and should probably be connected to an outfit

### 1. Deck Control & Draft Agency
Banishes: The ability to exclude specific cards from appearing in mid-run draft choices, narrowing the draft pool toward a preferred strategy.
Reroll Charges: A pool of mid-run rerolls for card rewards, relic chests, or event options.
Draft Weighting: Increasing the drop probability of unseen, locked, or newly unlocked cards and relics.
Card Purge Discounts: Reducing the gold cost or increasing the frequency of card removal services at shops and rest sites.

### 2. Early-Combat & Hybrid Stabilization
Innate Assignment: Allowing a player to mark a specific starter card to always appear in their opening hand on Turn 1.
Turn 1 Healthgain: Temporary health, healing, or lust reduction granted only on the first turn of combat to absorb initial setup damage while building an engine.
Opening Energy Buffers: Bonus energy granted exclusively during the first 1–2 turns of a fight.
Health Pool Padding: Direct increases to starting base HP, total Max HP caps, or emergency death-prevention shields (e.g., survive a lethal blow with 1 HP once per run).
Super Exhaust: Powerful single-time use cards which can carry a shaky party through a single tough moment but are removed from the deck afterwards.
Early Status Cleanses: Innate resistance or auto-cleansing for the first negative status condition inflicted on the party in a combat.

### 3. Economic & Resource Scaling
Capital Injection: Extra starting gold at the beginning of Act 1.
Bounty Multipliers: Bonus gold or extra card draft choices awarded specifically from Elite or Boss encounters.
Shop Restock & Discount Levers: Lowering overall merchant prices, unlocking extra purchase slots in shops, or allowing shop inventories to be refreshed.
Rest Site Efficiency: Enhancing rest node options, such as allowing rest sites to offer both healing AND card upgrades simultaneously, or offering additional services at the rest sites.

### 4. Situational Friction & Hazard Mitigation
Retaliation Dampening: Reducing reflected damage taken from enemy mechanics like Thorns, or counter-attacks.
Deck-Clog Resistance: Decreasing the frequency of, or automatically exhausting, junk cards (e.g., Dazed, Wounds, Spores) shuffled into the deck by enemies.
Position Lock: Resistance against forced movement, rank scrambling, or entangle effects inflicted by enemies.
Status Decay: Decreasing the duration or stat-penalty strength of negative debuffs (Weak, Vulnerable, Frail) applied to this or party members.

### 5. Situational Friction Reversal
Punishment as Reward: Transforming specific weaknesses of your party into advantages, like enemies gaining thorns gaining spikes instead.
Deck-Clog Tolerance: Abilities and upgrades for cards to have them consume cards in your hand for some mild benefit or draw.
Tax Transformation: Gaining benefits that outweigh the negatives when inflicted with negative debuffs like a squishy mage drawing cards when inflicted with weakness.

### 6. Synergy & Hook Multipliers
Status Application Boosts: Increasing the base stack count when applying open-ended status hooks (e.g., applying +1 extra stack of Poison).
Cross-Party Hand-Off Efficiency: Reducing the action point cost of starting cards or otherwise making them them less useless and more pallateable to a viable build.
Resource Refunds: Refunding a portion of Energy or Card Draw upon successfully consuming a status hook or executing a multi-card chain.
Engine Scaling Multipliers: Accelerating how fast passive scaling buffs (like Strength, Block-per-turn, or DoT ticks) build up during drawn-out encounters.

### 7. Growth Stage Benefits
Content Unlocks: Unlocking new rare cards and relics to appear in reward pools.
Collector's Boon: Increasing the chances of encountering unseen events and unobtained items/cards.
Growth Rate: Bonus to experience growth.
Ease of Obtaining Sub-Goals: Reduction to outfit & common relic/heirloom costs in the shop and other one-time purchase benefits which contribute to meta progression.
Weight Training: Reduction to combat effectiveness that will be punishing in the late game in exchange for early game strength, helping the player unlock more material.

# Item & Card Design Justifications

Every card, skill, item, and relic in the game must ask the following questions to justify its inclusion:

### 1. Starting: The Buffer
* Does this strengthen a hybrid build, rather than enabling specifically grind vs burst strategies?
* Does this add to a buffer that will give more time to assemble a burst or grind engine?
* Does this increase the player's ability to assemble an engine once they have chosen one?
* If this does lay the groundwork for a late game build, is it at least balanced by creating a very large vulnerability for the player to build around and solve, which will be just as interesting to build around and solve as working towards a burst vs grind engine?

This is where any option in the teambuilding scene goes. If the answer to all of the above is "No", it might not be a good fit as starting toolkit entry.

### 2. Early-game: Accelerate
* Does this directly fuel the momentum of a Burst combo?
* Does this contribute to the survivability of a Grind engine?
* Does this come with some kind of cost, such as opening a hole in one of the strategies (ie costing a resource that must be replaced, or working only in a fragile context), or requiring a specific condition to contribute to it's best-fit strategy?
* Does this's design avoid creating an "instant pick"/"dominant strategy" situation, reducing the desirability of rare cards?
* Is this card stronger than a fully upgraded basic card?

This section is for common things the player wants if they know what sort of strategy they're aiming for. They aren't meant to be exciting on their own, they are meant to raise the excitement and joy of rarer things. If the answer to all of the above is "No", it likely isn't a good early game item / common card.

### 3. Mid-game: Payoff
* Does this directly pay off the fuel a Burst combo builds up?
* Does this directly pay off a Grind deck's theoretically infinite resources?
* Does this have clear build-around potential, making it obvious the sort of playstyle which best suits this card?
* Does this help lead the player down either the Burst or Grind playstyle?
* Is this card stronger than a fully upgraded basic card?

This section is for rare things the player should be most excited to see, finally allowing them to commit or activating the strategy they've been missing an essential piece for. If the answer to all of the above is "No", it likely isn't a good rare item / card.

### 4. Late-game: Shores Up a Vulnerability
* Is the vulnerability this shores up one the player is likely to have? More specifically:
* Is the vulnerability one that the character who owns this creates themselves (e.g. self-damage for a reward creates a need for healing)?
* Does this give a Burst deck a way to survive friction designed to punish Burst strategies (e.g. reducing the punishing nature of drawing curse cards)?
* Does this give a Grind deck a way to survive friction designed to punish Grind strategies (e.g. reducing the fall-off rate of defenses)?

This section is for normally situational or highly specific items the player may see many times but only want once. They serve as chaff to make reward pools less overpoweringly good, but they should never serve as *just* chaff. If the answer to all of the above is a "No", then it isn't useful in highly niche scenarios.

# Quality Standards

It is essential to keep these rules in mind when designing anything in the game. Exceptions should be extremely, EXTREMELY rare and always completely justified by context.

* No Dead Weight: There are no "utility-only" or *purely* situational cards that do not contribute to one of the two core strategies. Every draft pick must serve the player's overarching operational goal or assist in assembling the tools to achieve that goal.

* Temporary, never boring: If a knowledgeable player does not think highly of a card, it must be because the are comparing it to something more exciting coming later. No card, item, outfit, or mechanic should be identified by experienced players as worthless. Any time a card seemingly has low value or complexity, it must be because it is hiding some unseen value or potential.

* No Pitfalls: The player should always know what they're getting themselves into. If building a maze, paths reaching a dead end should be clear that they are not worth exploring immediately.

* Never a Grind: Every single run the player experiences, at *least* one new thing should happen. One new unlock obtained, one new goal to pursue, one new challenge they don't know how to overcome. A completely repeated run is likely the last run that player will undertake.
